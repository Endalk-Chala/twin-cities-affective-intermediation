# AI Coding Pilot: 40-Item Diagnostic Report

## Scope
This diagnostic summarizes the completed 40-item AI-coded reliability pilot for **Affective Intermediation in Twin Cities Nonprofit Communication**. The pilot is balanced across 20 LinkedIn items and 20 website items.

## Coding confidence and ambiguity
- 30 of 40 items are coded `high` confidence.
- 10 of 40 items are coded `medium` confidence.
- 10 of 40 items carry an ambiguity flag.
- The medium-confidence and ambiguity cases coincide in this pilot, creating a clearly identifiable subset for manual review.

## Concept-pair diagnostics
Selected Pearson correlations across the 40-item pilot:

- Fear and anxiety / uncertainty: approximately **0.90**
- Anger and moral outrage: approximately **0.95**
- Solidarity and care / compassion: approximately **0.44**
- Care / compassion and empathy: approximately **0.59**
- Hope and reassurance: approximately **0.16**

### Interpretation
Fear and anxiety are empirically close, as are anger and moral outrage. The distinctions remain theoretically meaningful under the codebook: fear is threat-oriented while anxiety emphasizes uncertainty; anger need not include a normative judgment whereas moral outrage explicitly ties anger to injustice, abuse, rights violation, or wrongdoing. These pairs should remain separate in the coding scheme, but later statistical modeling should check multicollinearity and may use composite sensitivity analyses where appropriate.

Hope and reassurance appear empirically distinct in the pilot, supporting retention as separate categories. Hope is future-oriented; reassurance functions more specifically to reduce present fear or uncertainty.

## Platform comparison
Mean intensity by platform suggests preliminary differences, but the pilot is not intended as a population estimate.

Website items show higher average intensity than LinkedIn items for several crisis-oriented affects, especially moral outrage, anger, grief, fear, urgency, and empathy. LinkedIn items show somewhat higher hope, pride, and reassurance. Because the pilot was purposively constructed for reliability testing, these differences should be treated as diagnostic rather than substantive findings.

## Codebook sparsity and overlap assessment
No core emotion category is absent from the pilot. Gratitude and pride are relatively less frequent, but they appear in enough items to remain useful, especially for distinguishing routine organizational communication from acute crisis communication.

Solidarity and care are near-ubiquitous in the pilot. Their prevalence does not by itself indicate redundancy: solidarity codes relational identification and standing-with, while care codes protection, assistance, and concern for wellbeing. Their moderate correlation also supports retaining both.

## Recommendation before full-corpus coding
1. **Retain the current emotion taxonomy.** Do not merge fear/anxiety or anger/moral outrage at the coding stage.
2. **Freeze the 0–3 intensity definitions** unless manual reliability review identifies systematic disagreement.
3. **Manually review the 10 ambiguity/medium-confidence cases** as the highest-priority reliability subset.
4. **Preserve emotion source distinctions** when affect is attributed to clients, affected publics, quoted clinicians, or journalists rather than directly voiced by the organization.
5. **Use full-corpus analysis, not this pilot, for substantive claims about platform differences or temporal trajectories.**

## Analytical implication
The pilot supports the project’s central conceptual distinction: organizational communication frequently combines affect with appraisal, service, advocacy, reassurance, and action pathways. Solidarity and care appear as recurrent connective affects, while fear, outrage, grief, defiance, and urgency become more pronounced in acute enforcement and violence-related items. This pattern is consistent with treating affective intermediation as a process in which organizations translate community threat and vulnerability into communicative forms that can include care, solidarity, preparedness, advocacy, and mobilization.
